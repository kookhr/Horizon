## [Tsinghua Team Builds World's First Stable Nuclear Optical Clock](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11122-1) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A Tsinghua University research team has developed the world's first stable nuclear optical clock by using thorium-229 doped calcium fluoride crystals excited by a self-developed 148nm continuous-wave vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) laser, with results published in Nature. The clock stabilizes the laser to the thorium-229 nuclear isomeric transition using a rapid feedback loop based on continuous absorption spectroscopy. This represents a paradigm shift from traditional atomic clocks, which use electronic transitions, to nuclear clocks based on nuclear energy-level transitions that are far less sensitive to external perturbations. The breakthrough could enable next-generation time-frequency standards with unprecedented precision, benefiting satellite navigation, deep space exploration, and fundamental physics tests such as probing variations in fundamental constants. The thorium-229 nuclei are embedded in a millimeter-sized, room-temperature calcium fluoride crystal, and the clock operates at the 148.4nm nuclear transition corresponding to an isomer energy of approximately 8.35574 eV. The self-developed continuous-wave VUV laser is stabilized to this nuclear transition via a rapid feedback loop, achieving the first demonstration of a nuclear optical clock with stable operation.

**Background**: Nuclear clocks differ from conventional atomic clocks by using transitions within the atomic nucleus rather than electronic transitions as the timing reference, offering potentially higher stability because nuclear energy levels are shielded by the electron cloud and are far less sensitive to electromagnetic perturbations. Thorium-229 is uniquely suited for this purpose because its isomeric state has an exceptionally low excitation energy of approximately 8.4 eV, placing it in the vacuum ultraviolet range accessible to modern laser technology — the only known nucleus with such a low-energy transition. Realizing a nuclear clock has been a decades-long goal in the precision metrology community, requiring simultaneous breakthroughs in VUV laser technology, thorium-229 doping in solid-state hosts, and high-resolution nuclear spectroscopy.
