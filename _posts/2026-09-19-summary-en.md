## [Stanford study reveals two parallel brain progenitors, challenging single-origin model](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2026/09/two-separate-brains.html) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Researchers at Stanford Medicine identified two distinct neural ectoderm progenitors—anterior (forebrain/midbrain) and posterior (hindbrain)—that arise simultaneously during gastrulation, challenging the long-held single-progenitor model of brain development. The study, published in Nature Neuroscience, also yielded a new in vitro technique for culturing hindbrain stem cells, which had been notoriously difficult to grow. If validated, this dual-progenitor model fundamentally reshapes our understanding of brain development and could accelerate research into hindbrain-related diseases such as ALS, spinocerebellar ataxia, and certain brainstem tumors. The new in vitro cultivation technique is particularly significant because it removes a major bottleneck for studying posterior brain disorders in the lab. The study used lineage tracing in mouse embryos to demonstrate that the two progenitor populations are lineage-restricted from the earliest stages of gastrulation, rather than diverging from a common precursor. A free preprint is available on bioRxiv (July 2025) under a CC-BY 4.0 license, and community members noted that the actual findings are more nuanced than Stanford's press release framing of the brain as 'two separate organs.'

## [Terence Tao: Mathematics Is More Than Formal Proof](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/18/if-math-is-more-than-proof-we-need-to-better-celebrate-the-rest-of-it/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Terence Tao published a blog post arguing that mathematics encompasses far more than formal proof, and that the field must better recognize and celebrate its other dimensions—such as intuition, exposition, conjecture, and connection-making—particularly as AI increasingly automates proof generation. The post sparked extensive discussion with 231 comments exploring how the mathematical community should reevaluate what it values. As AI tools become capable of generating and verifying formal proofs, the traditional currency of mathematical achievement—rigorous proof—is being partially automated, forcing the community to confront what uniquely human contributions to mathematics remain. This has profound implications for how mathematicians are trained, evaluated, and rewarded, and parallels similar disruptions already underway in software engineering and other knowledge-work fields. Tao's argument touches on the historical tension between intuition and rigor in mathematics, echoing the early 20th-century debate between Poincaré and Hilbert, where Hilbert's emphasis on formalism ultimately dominated. Community commenters note that AI's impact may disproportionately affect elite mathematicians whose competitive advantage lay in raw problem-solving power, potentially narrowing the skill gap between top-tier and average practitioners.

**Background**: Formal proof in mathematics is a finite sequence of well-formed formulas, each being an axiom or derived via inference rules, establishing the truth of a theorem with complete rigor. Automated theorem proving (ATP) is a subfield of automated reasoning that uses computer programs to generate such proofs automatically, and has been a motivating factor in computer science since its inception. In recent years, AI-driven systems have made significant strides in proof assistance and generation, raising questions about the future role of human mathematicians. The mathematical community has historically prized formal proof above other mathematical activities, a norm that Tao suggests needs reexamination.
